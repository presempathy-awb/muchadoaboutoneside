01 · What we are asking for

Overview

One poem, written once by hand in a slanted script, becomes the endless inscription on both foil editions of the sculpture.

The sculpture is a figure-eight snake wrapped in aluminum foil. The poem “Much Ado About One Side” is laser-marked onto that foil as one continuous ribbon of script that runs along the body, around the nose, back along the other side, around the tail, and straight into its own first word. The website currently shows the poem in a substitute font called Great Vibes. Your hand-lettering replaces it.

We do not need you to letter the sculpture or the foil. We need a master: the complete poem written on paper at a comfortable size, with consistent guidelines, so it can be scanned, traced into vector outlines, and scaled to each edition. The master is written once and reused everywhere.

Where the lettering goes

EditionSize of the letteringHow the poem repeats
180 mm printed maquette (first physical run)2.5 mm em, x-height about 0.89 mm2 complete repetitions along one 1028 mm ribbon around the body
Full-size sculpture (conceptual study)Set after fit tests; the same outlines scale up4 rows around the body, each one complete poem on a closed reading path; the jaw carries its own 18-row inscription

Because the same outlines serve both editions, the master is drawn at one working size and every number in this guide is given in millimetres on your paper. The tables also show what each size becomes on the small maquette, which is the tightest case.

The deliverable in one sentence

23 labelled rows of black script on white paper, one poem line (or half line) per row, 7 mm x-height, 55° slant, no stroke thinner than 1 mm, scanned at 600 dpi.

Your hand, not the font

Great Vibes was chosen because its slanted script and flourished capitals resembled your sample. Treat the rendered reference sheets as a size and proportion guide only. Your letterforms, joins, and flourishes are what we want.

The poem, exactly

03Draw, O coward! cries the knight.04So inkhands draw him at halfheight.05That halfknight draws a quarterknight,06who draws a knightling, out of sight.07This swordfeud, quoth one, is accursed:08each little inkprick's like the first!09Take sides! they roar. 02 · From blank sheet to approved master

Step by step

Twelve steps, each with a check you can do before moving on. Most of the time goes into warming up and writing; the rest is checking.

  1. 01

    Read the poem aloud from the exact-text sheet

    Use the “The poem, exactly” page. Note the invented compound words (palindove, inkhands, halfheight, halfknight, quarterknight, knightling, swordfeud, doublelong, mirrorknave, swordwave), the apostrophes, and the single lowercase start of the last line. The wording is checked letter by letter against the source text.

    Check You can name every punctuation mark on the inventory table without looking.

  2. 02

    Gather materials

    One pen (brush pen recommended), its black pigment cartridge, smooth bright-white paper, ruler, 2H pencil, eraser, low-tack tape, and a scrap of the same paper for pen tests. The materials table lists specific products and what to avoid.

    Check Test-write “palindove” on the scrap; the ink dries matte black with no feathering.

  3. 03

    Print the template sheets at 100 %

    Print the eight ghosted sheets and at least ten blank ruled sheets. In the print dialog choose “Actual size” or 100 %, landscape, no “fit to page”. Print on the writing paper itself if the printer accepts it; otherwise print on plain paper and use it under your paper on a light pad.

    Check Measure the bar in each sheet's header: it must be 100 mm, within 1 mm.

  4. 04

    Warm up on the alphabet sheet

    Write the lowercase alphabet twice and the capitals once at the working size, joined as you would in running text. Aim the body of a, o, n at the 7 mm x-height, ascenders to the 12 mm line, descenders to the 6 mm line.

    Check Ten consecutive letters sit on the baseline and touch the x-height line without drifting.

  5. 05

    Set your stroke weights

    Write “Come, palindove,” at working size. Lay the printed stroke gauge beside the thinnest hairline: it must be at least 1 mm wide. Thick strokes should be 2–3 mm. If your hairlines are thinner, increase the minimum pressure or switch to the 1.0 mm marker for the master.

    Check No stroke in the test line is narrower than the 1 mm gauge bar.

  6. 06

    Practise the hard words

    Use the hard-words sheet. Write each compound word as one word with no gap and no hyphen. Then practise the capitals that appear in the poem (C, D, E, F, I, L, O, S, T, shown darker on the alphabet sheet) with the flourish you intend to use for each, staying inside the dotted flourish lines.

    Check Every flourish stays between the dotted ceiling and floor lines; nothing crosses into the next row.

  7. 07

    Write the master rows in order

    Use blank ruled sheets. One row per labelled row ID (R01 to R17, with a/b halves), in poem order, writing the row ID in pencil in the left label box. Keep one steady word gap of about 5–7 mm. Never split a word across rows. If a row runs long, stop at a word gap, write the rest on the next row, and label both with the same ID plus a and b.

    Check Each row starts at the left rule and ends before the right margin; the text matches the exact-text sheet.

  8. 08

    Let every sheet dry fully

    Brush-pen pigment is touch dry in a minute; sumi needs an hour. Do not stack sheets or erase before then.

    Check A clean tissue pressed on the darkest stroke picks up nothing.

  9. 09

    Review with the checklist

    Go row by row with the quick-reference checklist: wording, capitals, punctuation, x-height, slant, joins, stroke minimum, flourish zones, nothing touching the row above or below.

    Check Every row has a pencil tick in its label box.

  10. 10

    Rewrite, do not patch

    If a row has a wrong letter, a blob, a stroke that touches another row, or a hairline under 1 mm, rewrite the whole row on a fresh ruled sheet with the same ID. Put a single pencil X through the rejected row. Do not use correction fluid on a master row; a clean rewrite scans cleaner than a repair.

    Check Exactly one un-crossed row exists for each row ID.

  11. 11

    Scan or photograph every sheet

    Flatbed at 600 dpi, greyscale, no auto-contrast or sharpening, saved as TIFF or PNG named by sheet (for example jill-sheet-03.tif). Keep the whole sheet in the scan, including the row labels and the 100 mm bar, so scale and identity are recorded. If you must photograph: daylight, straight on, the whole sheet in frame, no shadows.

    Check The scanned bar measures 100 mm when the file is opened at 600 dpi.

  12. 12

    Approve the proof

    Andrew traces the scans into outlines, joins the rows into the continuous ribbon, and sends back a proof at 1:1 and at 4:1 alongside a maquette-size print. Mark anything you want changed. Nothing is laser-marked before you approve the proof, and the first laser job is a small test coupon, not the full skin.

    Check You have written “approved” and the date on the proof.

Keep the originals

Please keep the paper sheets flat and unsigned until the proof is approved. A signature or note can be added afterwards on a separate sheet if you would like it recorded with the archive.

Do

  • Write in your own slanted, joined script with flourished capitals.
  • Keep the x-height on the 7 mm line and the slant near 55°.
  • Keep the thinnest stroke at 1 mm or wider.
  • Keep flourishes inside the dotted lines.
  • Write compound words as single words: palindove, inkhands, halfknight.
  • Copy punctuation exactly, including the final ellipsis.
  • Label every row; rewrite rather than patch.

Don't

  • Don't add words, hyphens, or extra capitals.
  • Don't split a word across two rows.
  • Don't let any stroke touch the row above or below.
  • Don't use coloured, glossy, or metallic ink.
  • Don't crop the row labels or the 100 mm bar out of the scans.

Row checklist

  • Wording matches the exact-text sheet, letter for letter
  • Capitals only where the poem has them
  • Punctuation present and correct
  • Bodies of letters sit on the baseline and reach the x-height line
  • Slant consistent along the row
  • Thinnest stroke at least 1 mm
  • Flourishes stay inside the dotted lines
  • Row ID written in the label box

What to send

  1. 23 approved rows across the sheets, scanned at 600 dpi as TIFF or PNG, one file per sheet, with the full sheet in frame.
  2. One photo of the pen you used and the paper's packaging, so the archive records the materials.
  3. Any notes on rows you were unsure about.
05 · Why the numbers are what they are

In depth

Proportions, spacing and joins, flourish zones, what the laser can and cannot mark, materials in detail, how the scan becomes foil, and what to do when things go wrong.

Proportions

The layout is built around the em, the nominal row size. The stand-in font has an x-height of 0.36 em, ascenders at 0.62 em, capitals at 0.81 em, and descenders to 0.32 em. At the working size that gives a 7 mm x-height, 12 mm ascenders, 16 mm capitals and 6 mm descenders. Your natural proportions may differ a little; what matters is that they stay the same on every row, because rows are scaled to a common x-height when they are joined.

The working sheets and the jaw master space rows 2.4 ems apart, which is 48 mm at the working size. The dotted flourish lines mark the space a letter may use without touching a neighbouring row: up to 22 mm above the baseline and 16 mm below. The maquette's ribbon band is 10 mm tall for a 2.5 mm em, so the same limits apply there. The large body master currently stacks 4 rows only 1.4 ems apart; before that run Andrew re-spaces the body rows to the real extent of your capitals and descenders, so do not tighten your hand for it.

Spacing, joins, and slant

  • Letters within a word join in the usual running-script way; keep the spacing between joined letters even and fairly tight, roughly the width of a lowercase n between stems.
  • Words are separated by one steady gap of 5–7 mm, about one x-height. The gap between poem lines on the ribbon is doubled (10–14 mm); Andrew adds that when the rows are joined, so end each row after the final punctuation with no special gap.
  • Slant is 55° from the baseline, the classic copperplate angle, drawn faintly on every sheet. If your natural slant is a few degrees different, use yours and keep it constant.
  • The ribbon is one continuous line, so the tail of each row must be able to meet the head of the next at a word gap. Exit strokes may trail a little; an entry flourish on a capital may lead in a little; neither should be longer than one word gap.
  • The final ellipsis runs into the first word. Give the three dots a normal ending and let the first “C” carry whatever lead-in flourish you like; the join will read as a loop either way.

Capitals and flourishes

The poem uses these capitals: C, D, E, F, I, L, O, S, T. Each begins a line or a sentence except the lowercase “come” that starts the last line, which is deliberate: the poem loops, so its last line is a continuation. Flourished capitals are welcome. Keep flourishes clear of the words before and after them, and never above the ceiling or below the floor line. A flourish that crosses another letter becomes an unreadable blob at 2.5 mm.

Why the 1 mm stroke rule

The foil is 0.127 mm AlumaMark aluminum. A CO₂ laser darkens its coating rather than cutting it, and the marked line cannot be narrower than the beam's focused spot, roughly 0.1–0.2 mm. Your master is scaled by 0.128 to reach the maquette's 2.5 mm em, so a 1 mm stroke on paper becomes 0.13 mm on the foil, right at the limit of what survives. Anything thinner drops out or fills in. Thick strokes of 2–3 mm keep the script's contrast without closing up the counters of e, a, and o.

If you prefer a pointed dip pen

Copperplate hairlines from a Nikko G or Zebra G nib are 0.1–0.2 mm on paper and would vanish. If that is the hand you want, write it anyway and say so: Andrew will thicken every outline uniformly by about 0.4 mm on the paper scale before scaling. The hairlines then survive, but the contrast softens and the letters gain a little weight. A brush pen avoids that step entirely.

Matte black on smooth bright white scans as a clean two-tone image, which is what vector tracing needs. Glossy inks reflect the scanner lamp and produce grey speckles inside strokes; textured paper adds noise along every edge; cream paper lowers contrast. Bristol or a heavy laser paper also stays flat on the scanner glass.

From scan to foil

  1. Each scan is checked against its 100 mm bar and straightened using the printed baselines.
  2. The image is thresholded to pure black and white and traced into closed vector outlines, the same kind of outlines the current stand-in font produces.
  3. Each row is measured from its baseline and x-height lines and normalised to the 19.6 mm em, so rows written on different days still match.
  4. Rows are joined in poem order with one word gap inside a line and a double gap between lines, producing one continuous ribbon.
  5. The ribbon's length is compared with the stand-in layout (about 194 ems for one poem). Up to about 15 % difference is absorbed by adjusting word gaps and a small uniform scale; outside that we talk before changing anything.
  6. The ribbon replaces the font outlines in the generators for both editions: two repetitions around the maquette's 1028 mm body circuit, and one poem per row on the large study.
  7. You receive a proof. After approval, denhac staff mark a small test coupon with your lettering at 2.5, 4, 6, 8, 10 and 14 mm em sizes on the actual foil, and we look at it together before any full sheet is marked.

Edges feather or bleedAbsorbent or textured paper, or a wet dip-pen lineSwitch to smooth Bristol or 120 g laser paper; let the pen's own cartridge ink do the work.
Hairlines under 1 mmToo little pressure on the up-strokesSlow down on up-strokes and keep the brush tip slightly loaded; or use the 1.0 mm marker for the master.
Counters of e, a, o fill inThick strokes over 3 mm at this sizeEase the pressure on down-strokes; the gauge's 3 mm bar is the ceiling.
