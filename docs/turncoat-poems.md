# Turncoat slough poems

Candidates for the inscription on the shed skin of **Turncoat**, the sculpture study in which the living figure-eight snake stands on, under and inside its own continuous pale slough. Written on 2026-10-08 by Claude Fable 5.1 from Andrew's preprompt "Turncoat — poem preprompt for Fable" (2026-10-07), which asked for a companion to the living snake's poem with its own voice, built on the dramatic question *what does a creature owe the shape it had to leave?*

These are poem texts, not site versions. `shared/poem.ts` still defines only the two wordings of *Much Ado About One Side* for the living body. The finished ones have reading pages under `/poems` (`/poems/right-side-out`, `/poems/understudy`, `/poems/turncoats`; the first draft has none), listed in `shared/poems.ts`, which a test holds to these files. Nothing here is wired into the editions or the generators, and no scale map or word budget has been supplied, so every word count below is a poem count and physical fit is unverified until scales are measured. Like the rest of the project-authored poem text, they are MIT OR Apache-2.0.

## Right Side Out

The shed skin speaks to the creature that left it. Its two words "Hold still" open and close the poem: at the start they are what the skin did so the snake could crawl out of it, at the end they are what the skin offers the snake that now rests in its fold. "Turn" moves from *turned* (everted) to *you turned* to *it turns out*; "leave" from departing to letting remain; "keep" from wanting to keep the snake to keeping the shape it is in. The skin refuses the word *we*, which it has diagnosed as a ring.

Inscription note (default version): 260 whitespace-delimited words, title excluded, punctuation kept with its word. Natural rests at the six stanza breaks and the short full stops "Hold still.", "So I talk.", "So I watch.", "Mine don't shut.", "I won't say we." Longest words: blindness, Turncoat, stretched, tightens, eyelids; none should be split or shrunk. The line break after "lies" is deliberate and may be reflowed. The poem reads in ordinary order and needs no mirrored or underside lettering; if the slough's text is ever mirrored to show eversion, a complete unmirrored copy must remain. "We is a ring." is deliberate. The revision runs twenty words over the preprompt's provisional 180–240 range; if a scale count forces a cut, drop "What you saw was a stone." first, then the face line.

Alternate ending, replacing the last three lines:

```
is where you lie. I know what we does.
I'll say it from underneath, this once.
We.
Hold still.
```

The main ending withholds the word and offers only the act; the alternate says it with the danger known, so the final "Hold still" tightens as well as bears.

What changed between the draft and the default: a new line, "What they take for my face is the back of yours", from the model notes' everted hollow head and the hollow-face illusion; the eyelids moved into the body stanza and "They don't shut" became "Mine don't shut"; "You found a stone" became "What you saw was a stone"; "catch" became "snag"; "And yes" became "Of course"; "lies" now ends its line; the last stanza shortens line by line.

## Understudy

The skin still on the living snake speaks to the shed one. It formed underneath the old skin, pressed from its inside scale for scale, so it is the old skin's understudy in the literal sense, and it has learned the old skin's line. The creature is never addressed, only called *it*: it cannot see the skin it wears, it looks only at the skins it has left, and a milk is already starting behind its eyes. The understudy asks the opposite of the shed skin: not to be kept but to be left whole and looked at. "I'm on." closes the poem and runs back into "I'm the one on it now."

Inscription note: 195 whitespace-delimited words, title excluded. Natural rests at the six stanza breaks and at "I'm held.", "It looked at them once.", "I'm asking the opposite.", "I know the line." Longest words: underneath, starting, opposite, learning, already. The "You're" anaphora in the second stanza and the quoted "Hold still, you tell it" should stay intact. Ordinary reading order; no mirrored lettering needed. "Smaller ones in the coil" refers to the smaller open skin segments inside the floor coil of the study and names no number. Physical fit is unverified.

Alternate ending, replacing the last four lines, in which the understudy refuses the inherited role:

```
I'll be where you are. I won't hold it up.
Hold still, you tell it. I've heard you.
I know the line.
I won't say it.
```

## Turncoats

The two poems combined into one dramatic poem in two voices, written 2026-10-08 at Andrew's request with no length limit. The shed skin speaks flush left and calls the creature *you*; the skin still on the creature speaks indented and calls the creature *it* and the shed *you*. The two never share a pronoun for the creature, which is how a listener keeps them apart; each voice also names its own position in its first words. The shedding happens inside the poem: the creature finds the stone, the understudy asks for the line and gets it ("Hold still", now said to a coat), comes off mouth first in the shed's own opening words, and asks where to lie. The old shed, which refused *we* for a ring, joins the *we* of the floor, where the small torn sheds of the young creature lie; the new shed takes over the holding; and the look the understudy asked for turns out to come from whoever is walking round the sculpture reading the scales, since the creature cannot read the skin it wears and only ever looks at the floor. The last line, spoken by the lining, runs back into the first, so a second reading is the same poem one generation on, every speaker moved up one place.

Inscription note: 613 whitespace-delimited words, title excluded, 89 lines in 16 stanzas; 33 lines are the coat's. In print the coat's lines are indented (italics also works); on the slough the two voices can take different rows, weights, or stretches, but the poem reads in one ordinary order with no marking at all. Rests at every stanza break and at the one-line stanzas "Hold still.", "Is that the look?", "Something's reading me. Scale by scale.", "I'm the one on it now." Longest words: underneath, blindness, Turncoat, stretched. "I wear them turned" and "Nobody reads the back of a word" describe the creature's old lettering on the everted skin; they are images, not an instruction to mirror anything. "Cast from it" is meant three ways: moulded, a cast skin, a theatre cast. Physical fit is unverified; the two single poems remain for a smaller scale budget.

## Facts behind the images

Nothing in either poem is invented biology or invented geometry.

- The eye cap (spectacle) is a scale shed with the skin. Before a shed a lymph-like fluid separates its layers, so the eye goes bluish-white and sight drops for days; the eyes clear a few days before the skin comes off ([Veterinary Vision Center](https://veterinaryvisioncenter.com/spectacular-diseases-in-snakes/), [Iowa DNR](https://www.iowadnr.gov/news-release/2015-08-25/why-do-snakes-shed-their-skin)). The spectacle is the fused, transparent eyelid, which is why "eyelids" that "don't shut" is fair.
- The split opens around the mouth after the snake rubs its snout on something rough; the skin is snagged, and comes off inverted in one piece. A healthy shed is one piece.
- A shed is longer than its snake because it stretches on the way off; a measured average is about 11 percent, keepers report more ([Zoological Studies](https://zoolstud.sinica.edu.tw/Journals/56/56-38.pdf)).
- Retained skin "acts as a tourniquet on the tail tip and digits leading to ischaemic necrosis" ([WikiVet](https://en.wikivet.net/Special:MobileDiff/55676...97355), [Reptiles Magazine](https://reptilesmagazine.com/diagnosing-and-treating-dysecdysis-aka-retained-shed/)).
- The new skin forms beneath the old one before the shed, which is the understudy's "pressed from your inside".
- A concave mask reads as a convex face from a distance even with both eyes open, strongest for upright faces ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4934646/)). The study's shed head is an everted hollow cap with cut eye windows and a split lower jaw.
- The study's slough is one continuous shell: a fitted sheath near tail and mouth, a free sleeve above the head, a long descending support, a fallen figure eight on the floor and a cradle around the standing snake's lower lobe. Two smaller open skin segments lie inside the floor coil. (Turncoat model notes, 2026-10-07, in Andrew's sculpture-studies folder; not in this repository.)

## Literary notes

- *Much Ado About Nothing* 1.1: Beatrice says courtesy itself must convert to disdain in Benedick's presence; he answers, "Then is courtesy a turncoat." The sculpture's name is already in the play the project title puns on. A possible epigraph for a site page, not for the scales ([text](http://shakespeare.mit.edu/much_ado/much_ado.1.1.html)).
- *A Midsummer Night's Dream* 2.1: "there the snake throws her enamell'd skin, / Weed wide enough to wrap a fairy in", where *weed* means a garment ([Poems on the Underground](https://poemsontheunderground.org/from-a-midsummer-nights-dream)).
- Lucretius, *De rerum natura* 4: "cum lubrica serpens / exuit in spinis vestem; nam saepe videmus / illorum spoliis vepres volitantibus auctas", the slippery serpent strips its coat on the thorns and the brambles are enriched with the fluttering spoils ([Latin Library](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/lucretius/lucretius4.shtml)). "I'm the coat" and "snag on the bark and hold" have an old warrant.
- Etymology: *turncoat* is recorded from the 1550s, from the image of reversing a coat to hide or change a badge of allegiance; the Duke of Saxony anecdote is a later folk story ([Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/turncoat)).
- No published poem spoken by a shed skin to its snake was found. The nearest, Liz Beasley's "Snakeskin" (*Poetry*, 2001), is observed from outside; its text could not be fetched, so that echo check is unverified.
